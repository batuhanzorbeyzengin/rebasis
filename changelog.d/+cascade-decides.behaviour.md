`probe` now recommends the two-stage arrangement, and prints what it costs.

The largest measured win in this repository was one the tool measured, served and did not recommend. [`docs/cascade-band.md`](https://batuhanzorbeyzengin.github.io/rebasis/cascade-band/) puts single-stage bridging beating the status quo in **1 run of 48** and a two-stage arrangement — the bridge fetches candidates, the new model reranks them in its own space — in **36**. `rebasis.serve.Cascade` has served that arrangement since 0.1. A user ran `probe`, read `full_reindex`, and left.

What blocked the recommendation was a price rather than a doubt: the arrangement re-embeds N documents per query, and how many of those are already cached is a property of your traffic rather than of your corpus. `probe` reads a corpus.

**That objection does not survive `--queries`.** A real query log is a sample of the traffic, and what sets the hit rate is how much the candidate sets overlap between queries — countable off a search the run already ran, at no extra cost. `probe` counts it as `candidate_reuse` and reports it as the lower bound it is: a running cache accumulates across queries a sample does not contain, so the figure understates the real hit rate, which is the direction a price used in a decision has to err in.

So `--json` gains `arrangement` (`single_stage` or `cascade`), `candidate_reuse`, `cascade_embeddings_per_query` and `cascade_n`; the report gains a block of its own rather than a footnote under a heading that says "rebuild your index"; and the terminal prints the `fit` command and the `Cascade(...)` call to go with it. `decision` keeps its five values — a sixth would break every script branching on that field, and the two are different axes anyway: one says what to do with the index, the other what to put in front of it.

The rule fires only when all four of its conditions hold: the two-stage figure clears the ±0.025 band, the single stage does not already win, the store returns document text (`Cascade` refuses at construction without it, so recommending the arrangement there would name something nobody can run), and the reuse was actually measurable. Where the first two hold and one of the last two does not, the run says so instead of staying quiet.

**The candidate depth moves from 100 to 200**, in `probe` and in `Cascade` together, and `--cascade-n` exposes it. While the number only decorated a report, 100 was the smaller claim and half the hot-path cost. It now decides, and the 36 of 48 was measured at 200.

Both measurements behind this are published, including the one the rule loses. On accuracy the rule scores 35 of 48 against 36 of 48 for a rule that ignores its inputs and always recommends the arrangement — which is the expected shape when 75% of outcomes fall one way, and the reason [section 9](https://batuhanzorbeyzengin.github.io/rebasis/bridge-band/#9-what-the-counting-is-worth) stopped trusting proportions. What the rule is for is naming runs that will win: of the 23 it named, **23 won**, against the constant rule's 36 of 48. It orders the runs by the margin they returned at Spearman ρ = +0.939, and the runs it selected returned a mean margin of +0.446 against +0.035 for the rest (permutation test, p = 0.0001). The identity check that section 9 exists for was run first: `cascade_advantage` and the outcome differ by up to 0.59 and agree on none of the 48 runs, so it is a prediction rather than the same inequality written twice.
